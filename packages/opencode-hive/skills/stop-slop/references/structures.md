# Structures to examine

## LinkedIn cadence

A thought-leadership register uses line breaks and reversals as the argument. It can survive even after filler phrases are gone.

- One sentence per paragraph uses white space as emphasis.
- "I used to think X. Then I learned Y" can turn a generic lesson into a manufactured anecdote.
- "Stop X. Start Y" substitutes a couplet for the action.
- "That's it. That's the whole secret" performs simplicity.
- Numbered "lessons" with no operational content manufacture listicle profundity.
- "The best X don't Y. They Z" stages an authority contrast.

Write continuous prose. Keep a personal anecdote when it supplies a fact the reader needs. Do not invent or erase that person's experience to improve cadence.

## Antithesis and stacked negation

"Not because X. Because Y", "This isn't about X. It's about Y", and "Not X. Not Y. Z" create predictable reversals. Joining the fragments into "not because X, but because Y" leaves the same frame.

Other forms of the same move:

| Pattern | Problem |
|---|---|
| "[X] isn't the problem. [Y] is." | Formulaic reframe |
| "The answer isn't X. It's Y." | Predictable pivot |
| "It feels like X. It's actually Y." | Setup/reveal cliche |
| "The question isn't X. It's Y." | Rhetorical misdirection |
| "It's not X, and it's not Y, but it is Z." | Stacked negation |
| "No X, no Y, just Z." | Tricolon denial |
| "X rather than Y" used as a punch | Contrast standing in for the fact |

State the claim directly, keeping the affirmative half of a collapsed contrast: "labels show intent rather than final copy" becomes "labels show intent". A denied item stays only when it passes the boundaries test in `writing-for-humans`. An instruction, source quotation, or design trade-off can legitimately depend on contrast.

## Dramatic fragmentation

"Speed. Quality. Cost", "X. And Y. And Z", and "This unlocks something. Freedom" can manufacture profundity. Complete sentences let the content do the work. A single deliberate fragment is not a defect on its own.

## Rhetorical setups

"What if I told you", "Here's what I mean", and "Think about it" may announce the point rather than deliver it. Keep questions that invite a real decision or investigation; remove staged question-and-answer reveals.

## Rhythm

Keep the number of items the content needs. Do not pad or trim a list to create or avoid a triplet. Vary paragraph endings and sentence lengths without regularizing a supplied author's voice. Punctuation follows `writing-for-humans`, not a separate ban here.

| Pattern | Fix |
|---|---|
| "By the time X, I was Y" as a repeated narrative template | Use the actual chronology without a formula |
| "X that isn't Y" | State the actual property directly when that preserves the claim |
| Questions answered immediately | Let questions breathe or cut the staged setup |
| Paragraphs repeatedly starting with "So" | Start with content |
| Sentences starting with "Look," | Remove the announcement |
| Every paragraph ends punchily | Vary endings |
| Em-dashes before reveals | Remove the staged reveal; apply the core punctuation policy |

## Aphorism formulas

Watch for "X is the language of Y", "X is the currency of Z", "X is not a tool but a mirror", and "X becomes a trap when". A reusable formula can sound profound without adding precision. State the claim it was hiding, but do not invent an empirical result to replace it.

Before: "The guide says consistent layouts help users predict behavior. Consistency is the currency of trust."

After: "The guide says consistent layouts help users predict behavior."

## Section restatements

"In this section we will", "In summary", "In conclusion", "Overall", and "To conclude" often recap a heading instead of adding information. End on the last new fact. Keep a summary when the reader needs one, not as automatic section chrome.
